
------- MODULE 7: COMMON LAW ---------
## Main Points
### Overview
- Contract law is governed by
	- Common Law in most of Canada (except Quebec, which has a Civil Code)
	- Relevant statutes
		- For example, Sale of Goods Act, Consumer Protection Act, Employment Standards Act, etc.
- A contract is an enforceable agreement between two or more parties that sets out rights, responsibilities, and liabilities
	- Can be written, oral, or partly written/oral
### Essential Elements of a Contract
- Consensus (Offer and Acceptance)
	- An offer is a proposal by the offeror
	- Acceptance must be unconditional
		- Any change is a counter-offer that rejects the original offer
	- Communication can be written, verbal, or by conduct
- Consideration
	- Each party must exchange something of value (money, goods, promises, or giving up a right)
	- Does not have to be of equal value
- Capacity
	- Parties must have legal capacity (age of majority, mental capacity, not intoxicated, etc.)
- Legality
	- The contract's purpose must be lawful and not against public policy
- Intention
	- Parties must intend to create legal relations
### Formation and Forms of Contracts
- Bilateral
	- Each side promises something to the other
- Unilateral
	- One party makes a promise that the other can accept by performing an act 
		- For example, reward offers
- Option Contracts
	- Keep an offer open for a set time in exchange for consideration
- Letters of Intent
	- Express intention to form a contract in the future, but can be risky if they are (mis)interpreted as binding
### Voiding a Contract
- Several doctrines allow a contract to be set aside
	- Mistake
		- Mutual misunderstanding of a fundamental term
	- Misrepresentation
		- Fraudulent
			- Intentional false statement
		- Negligent
			- Carelessly made false statement
		- Innocent
			- Made in good faith but is still untrue
	- Duress
		- Improper pressure or coercion that vitiates consent
	- Unconsciounability
		- Extreme inequality of bargaining power leading to an unfair contract
	- Frustration
		- Unforeseen even that makes performance impossible or fundamentally different
			- For example, destruction of subject matter
### Breach of Contract
- Fundamental Breach
	- Goes to the root of the contract
		- Innocent party may terminate and sue, or continue and sue for damages
- Simple Breach
	- Less serious
	- Contract remains valid, but the breaching party can be sued for damages
- Repudiation
	- When one party indicates they will not (or cannot) perform
- Anticipatory Breach
	- One party explicitly says they will breach before performance is due
### Remedies for Breach
- Damages (monetary)
	- Causation
		- Loss must be caused by the breach
	- Remoteness
		- Only losses reasonably foreseeable at the time of contract formation
	- Mitigation
		- Innocent party must take reasonable steps to reduce their loss
	- Types
		- Direct, consequential, liquidated damages (if genuine pre-estimate)
- Specific Performance
	- Court orders breaching party to perform as promised
		- Rare in construction contexts
- Injuction
	- Court restrains a party from breaching or continuing a breach
- Declaratory Order
	- Court declares rights and obligations
#### Measuring Damages
- Cost of Performance
	- Often used if the breaching party must fix or complete something
- Diminution in Value
	- Used if fixing the problem would be an excessive cost compared to the value gained
### Performing Under Protest
- If the other side demands extra work outside the original scope, you may
	- Refuse and risk the court ruling it was in-scope
	- Perform under protest (in jurisdictions allowing it) to preserve your right to claim extra payment later
### Amendments, Waiver, and Estoppel
- Amendments require fresh consideration and both parties' agreement
- Waiver
	- One party chooses not to enforce a right
	- Can be done by words or conduct
- Estoppel
	- If one party relies on another's waiver, the original right-holder may be "estopped" from going back on their promise
### Standard Clauses to Consider
- Parties
	- Identify all contracting parties clearly
- Scope of Work
	- Detailed or performance-based, depending on the contract type
- Time
	- "Time is of the essence" leads to strict enforcement of deadlines
- Changes
	- Mechanisms for change orders and cost/time adjustments
- Damages & Bonuses
	- Liquidated damages or penalty clauses (penalties are often unenforceable if not a genuine pre-estimate)
- Warranty
	- Defines time and scope of corrective obligations
- Indemnification
	- Shifts risk of losses/liabilities between parties
- Exclusion/Limitation
	- Excludes or limits remedies (must be clearly worded)
- Termination
	- Outlines how the contract ends
		- For example, completion, breach, mutual agreement
- Dispute Resolution
	- For example, negotiation, mediation, arbitration, or litigation
### Interpreting Contracts
- Courts look for the objective intention of the parties, using
	- Plain Meaning of words
	- Context and entire contract reading
	- Special meaning if industry-specific
	- Contra proferentem
		- Ambiguities interpreted against the drafter
### Types of Payment & Delivery Methods
- Payment Methods
	- Fixed Price
		- Lump sum
		- Extras for scope changes
	- Cost Plus
		- Actual costs + fee
		- Variants include guaranteed maximum price or target price with gain/pain share
	- Unit Price
		- Payment by measured quantities (common in earthworks)
- Delivery Methods
	- Design-Bid-Build
		- Separate design and build contracts
	- Design-Build
		- Single entity does both design and build
	- Design-Build-Operate or Design-Build-Finance-Operate
		- Contractor also operates (and possible finances) over a period
	- Construction Management
		- Owner hires a construction manager instead of a single main contractor
### Tendering
- Submitting a tender (a formal bid) can create two contracts
	- Contract A
		- Between the owner and each bidder, governing the tendering process
	- Contract B
		- Awarded contract with the chosen bidder
- Mistakes in a bid may or may not allow withdrawal, depending on whether they are obvious and material
- Bid shopping is frowned upon (using one bid to negotiate a lower price from another bidder)

------- MODULE 8: TORT and TORT LIABILITY ---------
## Main Points
### Introduction to Torts
- A tort is a civil wrong where one party's wrongful act/omission causes loss or harm to another
- Tortfeasor
	- The person/entity committing the tort
- Common remedies
	- Damages (monetary compensation)
#### Key Early Case
- Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932)
	- Established the modern duty of care principle
		- Must take reasonable care to avoid foreseeable harm to others
### Types of Torts
#### Unintentional Torts
- Negligence
	- Failing to meet the required standard of care
	- Must prove
		- Duty of care
		- Breach of that duty (standard of care not met)
		- Damage or loss
		- Causation (breach caused the loss, and it was not too remote)
- Negligent Misrepresentation
	- Making a statement carelessly, leading someone to suffer economic loss because they relied on it
#### Intentional Torts
- Fraud (Deceit)
	- Knowing misrepresentation intended to induce another to act
- Trespass
	- Unauthorised entry onto land
- Nuisance
	- Unreasonable interference with someone's use/enjoyment of property (often strict liability)
- Defamation
	- Harmful false statements damaging another's reputation
### Specific Tort Concepts
- Proximate Cause (Remoteness)
	- Damages must be linked to the tortious act
- Pure Economic Loss
	- Generally not recoverable unless it falls under specific exceptions
		- For example, negligent misrepresentation, or dangerous defects
- Fiduciary Duty
	- A heightened duty requiring the fiduciary to put the beneficiary's interests above their own
		- For example, power of attorney
### Professional Duty of Care for Engineers
- Even absent a direct contract, a professional can owe a duty of care if it is reasonably foreseeable that someone would rely on their work
- Examples
	- Surrey v. Carroll-Hatch
		- Soil engineer had no direct contract with owner but was still found liable for failing to warn the owner of soil issues
	- Edgeworth v. N.D. Lea
		- Engineering firm owed a duty of care to bidders relying on design/specifications
### Vicarious Liability
- An empower or principal can be liable for torts committed by employees/agents in the course of their work
- Example
	- Dutton v. Bognor Regis (1972), the employer of a negligent building inspector was held liable
### Concurrent Tortfeasors and Joint & Several Liability
- Concurrent Tortfeasors
	- Multiple parties share liability
		- For example, District of Surrey v. Carroll-Hatch
			- Both architect and engineer were partially liable for building settlement issues
- Joint and Several Liability
	- A victim can recover 100% of damages from any at-fault party, even if that party is only partially responsible, who can then seek contribution from the others
### Product Liability
- Manufacturers owe a duty of care to end users, even without a direct contract
	- For example, Donoghue v. Stevenson
- Defects
	- Manufacturing defect
	- Design defect
	- Failure to warn (duty to warn about dangers)
- Canada generally requires proof of negligence (not strict liability as in some US jurisdictions)
### Limitation Periods
- Vary by province
	- In Ontario
		- Basic
			- 2 years from discoverability of the claim
		- Ultimate
			- 15 years from the act/omission giving rise to the claim
- Contracts can sometimes alter these periods in business (non-consumer) contexts

## Main Points
### Risk and Responsibility
#### Risk
- Definition
	- Possibility of suffering a loss (involves uncertainty and potential consequences)
- Elements
	- Description of current conditions leading to loss
	- Description of the loss/consequence
- Risk Management Paradigm
	- Identify
	- Analyse (qualitative & quantitative)
	- Plan responses
	- Track/Control
#### Shifting/Transferring Risk
- Methods
	- Insurance
	- Performance bonds
	- Indemnities
	- Contract clauses
		- For example, liquidated damages
- Engineer's Caution
	- Avoid inadvertently accepting risk that belongs to the client
#### Disclaimers
- Purpose
	- Exclude or limit liability via contract clauses
- Enforceability
	- Courts interpret strictly
		- Cannot absolve "gross negligence"
- Examples
	- Consequential-damage exclusion
	- No-damage-for-delay
	- Pay-when-paid
### Dispute Resolution
#### Main Methods
- Litigation
	- Formal court process
		- Risk, expensive, time-consuming
	- Pleadings, discovery, trial
- Arbitration
	- "Private" trial; arbitrator acts like a judge
	- Can be binding or non-binding, faster than litigation
- Negotiation
	- Informal, cooperative discussion to each compromise
	- Less costly, preserves relationships
- Mediation
	- Neutral third party to facilitate settlement
	- Mediator does not impose a decision but helps parties find common ground
#### Key Litigation Concepts
- Statement of Claim
	- Lists plaintiff's allegations & relief sought
- Statement of Defence
	- Denies or counter plaintiff's allegations
- Discovery
	- Exchange of documents, interrogatories, examinations
- Expert Witnesses
	- Provide opinions on technical matters
### Dispute Avoidance
- Client Dissatisfaction
	- Major cause of lawsuits
- Preventative Measures
	- Clear communication of potential risk
	- Manage client expectations with milestones
	- Keep thorough records (diaries, emails, photos)
### Conflict Resolution
#### Conflict
- Often arises from schedules, resources, scope disputes
- Address quickly, ideally in private
#### Techniques
- Confronting/Problem-Solving
	- Win-win, collaborative
- Compromising
	- Each side concedes something
- Smoothing
	- De-emphasise disagreements to maintain harmony
- Withdrawal
	- Temporary avoidance
- Forcing
	- Imposing a solution
		- Often harmful to relationships

## Main Points
### Insurance
#### Definition
- Legal contract transferring risk from policyholder to insurer
- Two primary categories for engineering/construction
	- Liability Insurance
		- For example, Commercial General Liability, Errors & Omissions
	- Property Insurance
		- For example, Builders' Risk
#### Policy Terminology
- Coverage Period
	- Dates policy is in effect
- Deductible
	- Amount paid by insured before coverage
- Claims-Made vs. Occurrence
	- Claims-Made
		- Covers claims filed during policy period
	- Occurrence
		- Covers events that happen during policy period
### Insurance Issues
- Operating Without Insurance
	- High risk
		- Personal assets could be exposed
- Duties to Defend & Indemnify
	- Insurer pays legal fees (duty to defend)
	- Insurer pays valid claims (duty to indemnify)
- Subrogation
	- Insurer "steps into the shoes" of insured to sue third parties at fault
- Material Non-Disclosure
	- Failing to disclose relevant info can void the policy
- Denial of Coverage
	- Insurer may claim the event is outside policy scope
### Bonds
#### Definition
- Three-party contract
	- Principal (contractor)
	- Obligee (owner)
	- Surety (bonding company)
- Surety guarantees principal's performance or payment
#### Bond vs. Insurance
- Bonds
	- Surety can seek reimbursement from principal
- Insurance
	- Insurer absorbs covered loss
### Types of Bonds
- Performance Bond
	- Guarantees specified work performance up to "face-value" (often 50-100% of contract)
	- Surety has several options if principal defaults (completing work, paying bond amount)
- Payment Bond
	- Ensures subcontractors and suppliers get paid
	- Protects owner from liens if contractor fails to pay subs
- Bid Bond
	- Protects owner if winning bidder refuses to sign the contract
	- Face value usually ~10% of bid price
- Fidelity Bond
	- Protects employer from employee fraud/theft
	- Coverage often matches the property/money at risk

## Main Points
### Lien
- Definition
	- A lien is a claim against property, typically for unpaid services or materials provided to improve that property
	- Construction (or builders') lien
		- Allows contractors, subcontractors, or suppliers to claim against the improved property if not paid
- Enforcing a Lien
	- Making a Lien
		- Must register a claim for lien in the appropriate land registry office within strict time limits
		- A lien is only a claim until proven in court (perfected)
	- Perfecting a Lien
		- Requires starting a lawsuit within a set time or the lien expires
		- The court decides whether the lien is valid and the amount owed
	- Substitute Lien Authority
		- Third parties who buy or lend money against a property that has a lien on it generally rank below the lien claimant
	- Trust Provisions
		- Funds received by the contractor from the owner are held "in trust" for subcontractors and suppliers
		- Contractor must not use these funds for anything but the project's debts
	- Holdbacks
		- By statute, the owner must withhold a certain percentage (for example, 10%) from each progress payment to protect against liens
	- Risk Issues
		- Contractors can demand subcontractors post performance bonds or sign statutory declarations confirming all sub-trades are paid
### Delay
- Type of Delays
	- Concurrent Delays
		- Two or more delays happen at the same time, making it difficult to assign blame or award compensation
	- Compensable vs. Excusable
		- Compensable
			- The delaying party may owe money
				- For example, owner-caused delays
		- Excusable
			- May allow more time but not necessarily money
				- For example, weather
- No-Damages-for-Delay Clause
	- Contract wording that attempts to exclude owner liability for certain delays
	- Often enforceable if unintentional delays, but courts interpret strictly
- Acceleration (Fast-Tracking)
	- Constructive Acceleration
		- Occurs if a contractor is denied a valid time extension and is forced to speed up work at extra cost
	- Contractor may claim additional compensation if forced to accelerate under protest
### Impact Claims
- Definition
	- Claims for added costs due to inefficiencies arising from delays, interferences, or changes in work sequence
- Proving an Impact Claim
	- Show Liability (someone caused the impact)
	- Show Damages (extra costs incurred)
- Heads of Damage
	- Additional labour, travel, site overhead, head office overhead, loss of productivity, extended bonding or warranties, etc.
- Documentation & Methods
	- Total Cost Method
		- Actual cost minus estimated cost = damages (must prove no other causes)
	- Measured Mile Method
		- Compare the productivity of an unaffected portion of work to the affected portion

## Main Points
### Property Law
- Real vs. Personal Property
	- Real Property
		- Land and anything attached (buildings, fixtures)
	- Personal Property
		- Everything else, divided into tangible (physical items) and intangible (for example, intellectual property)
- Subcategories
	- Mines, Minerals, Oil, and Gas
		- Typically reserved to the government; licenses are needed
	- Multiple Ownership
		- For example joint tenants vs. tenants in common
	- Property Boundaries
		- Lateral support, riparian rights, and air rights can all affect ownership and usage
### Intellectual Property
- Copyright
	- Protects original literary, musical, artistic, or software works
	- No registration required in Canada
		- Lasts 50 years after the author's death
- Patent
	- Protects inventions that are new, useful, and inventive
	- Registration is lengthy and costly
		- Covers 20 years from filing date
- Trademarks
	- Protect words, logos, or symbols that distinguish goods/services
	- Must be registered
		- Renewable every 10 or 15 years (depending on jurisdiction)
- Industrial Design
	- Protects the shape, pattern, or ornamentation of mass-produced items
	- Registration required
		- Typically 10-year protection
- Trade Secrets
	- Confidential information with business value, protected as long as it remains secret (no formal registration)
### Internet Law
- Jurisdiction Issues
	- The internet has no clear geographic boundaries, so determining which court/law applies can be complex
- Torts Online
	- Includes defamation (for example, online libel), negligent misrepresentation, or infringement
	- Internet Service Providers (ISPs) can be liable if they actively participate in infringing actions
- E-Contracts
	- Electronic signatures often valid, but best practice is to specify acceptance rules (time of delivery, receipt)
	- "Click-through" or "browse-wrap" agreements can be binding if clearly displayed
- Privacy and Security
	- Personal data collection triggers compliance with privacy laws (e.g. PIPEDA in Canada)
- Domain Names and Cybersquatting
	- Owners must actively protect their trademarks or risk losing them to squatters

-------- 13 --------
## Main Points
### Introduction
- Historical Context
	- Environmental protection is a longstanding concern
		- For example, prohibitions on burning sea coal in 13th-century London
	- Modern legislation and common law have grown more complex over time to address broader environmental challenges
- Tort and Contract Law
	- Traditional torts (nuisance, negligence, trespass) can apply to environmental harm
	- Many modern environmental statutes supplement or override common law remedies
### Environmental Site Assessment (ESA)
- Definition & Guideline
	- ESAs follow CSA Z768 as a minimum professional standard
	- Typically voluntary but strongly recommended because of provincial requirements to report contamination
- Phased Approach
	- Phase I
		- Information gathering (no physical testing)
	- Phase II
		- Physical sampling (soil, groundwater)
	- Phase III
		- Detailed delineation of contamination + remediation plan
- Key Purpose
	- Identify environmental liabilities for buyers/sellers
		- For example, real estate transactions
	- Recommend cleanup or confirm site suitability
### Environmental Audit
- Purpose
	- Systematic check of compliance with environmental laws or internal standards
	- May help establish defences if prosecuted or sued
- Definition
	- "A systematic process of objectively obtaining and evaluating evidence regarding a verifiable assertion about an environmental matter"
- Common Goals
	- Identify risks and potential liabilities
	- Prevent or correct compliance issues
### Remedies for Private Landowners
- Contamination on Own Property
	- Possible contract claims or tort against sellers
		- For example, negligent misrepresentation
- Contamination from Neighbours
	- Trespass, nuisance, negligence, or strict liability claims (Rylands v. Fletcher)
	- Liability attaches if contamination seeps or flows from a neighbouring property
### Governmental Regulation
- Key Federal and Provincial Statutes
	- Fisheries Act, Canadian Environmental Protection Act, Transportation of Dangerous Goods Act, etc.
	- Provincial statues may require cleanup, impose fines, or regulate discharges
- Criminal Offenses
	- Serious violations can lead to fines or imprisonment
	- Due diligence is a potential defence
		- For example, internal audits, corrective plans, proper oversight
### Environmental Assessment Process
- Canadian Environmental Assessment Act
	- Requires EA (environmental assessment) for certain projects
	- Public review or input often included
	- Different levels
		- Screening, comprehensive study, mediation, or panel review
### Sustainable Development
- Canadian Efforts
	- Federal Sustainable Development Act (2008) + periodic Federal Sustainable Development Strategy
- Global Context
	- United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (2015-2030) emphasise social, economic, and environmental balance

## Main Points
### Introduction to OH&S
- Purpose
	- Protect workers from injury
	- Provide compensation through Workers' Compensation Boards (no-fault system)
- Fundamental Worker Rights
	- Right to know about workplace hazards
	- Right to participate in health/safety committees
	- Right to refuse dangerous work without repercussion
### Contracts and OH&S
- Prime Contractor Concept
	- One party designated to have overall responsibility for on-site safety
	- If none named, responsibility defaults to the owner
- Clauses in Construction Contracts
	- May require fencing, signage, or site-specific safety measures
	- Often include hold-harmless or indemnity clauses relating to personal injury
### Workers' Compensation
- Historic Trade-Off
	- Workers give up the right to sue employers in exchange for guaranteed compensation
	- Employers pay into a general fund that covers workplace injuries
- Federal vs. Provincial
	- Most workplaces governed by provincial workers' comp legislation
	- Federal legislation covers federally regulated sectors
### Occupational Health and Safety Processes
- Federal
	- Canada Labour Code + Canada Health & Safety Regulations govern federal workplaces
- Provincial
	- Each province has its own OH&S framework
	- Site inspections and compliance orders can be issued by safety officers
### Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System (WHMIS)
- What is WHMIS?
	- Nationwide system for classifying and communicating hazards of materials used in workplaces
- Key Elements
	- Labels
		- Hazard symbols, warnings, first-aid info
	- Safety Data Sheets (SDS/MSDS)
		- Detailed chemical and hazard data
	- Worker Education
		- Employers must train staff on safe handling procedures
- Classes of Controlled Products
	- Six main classes
		- For example, Class B for flammables, Class D for toxic materials, etc.
### Technical Standards and Safety Authority (TSSA)
- Role
	- Non-government organisation enforcing technical and safety services (elevators, boilers, fuel systems)
	- Governed by the TSSA Act, 2000
- Areas Covered
	- Amusement rides, propane/natural gas handling, pressure vessels, and more
